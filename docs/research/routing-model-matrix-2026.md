# Route each narrow task to its specialist

As of 26 September 2026, the evidence supports a two-part design for a desktop router. First, a small group of **Qwen-family models run locally through Ollama (preferably its `-mlx` tags)** can handle most text work on a 24–48 GB Mac. Second, **one OpenRouter key obtained through PKCE** covers every hosted modality: chat, images, audio input and output, video input, embeddings and reranking. Hosted general models fall into three clear price tiers. The fast/cheap tier is **GPT-6 Luna ($0.10/$0.50 per 1M tokens)**, the Gemini Flash-Lite and Flash models, and the DeepSeek V4 Flash models. The workhorse tier is **Claude Sonnet 5 and GPT-6 Sol (both $2/$10)**. The frontier tier is led by **Claude Opus 5.5, which scores 58 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at $4/$20**. For media, small dedicated models beat large general ones. A 0.9B OCR model outscores a 235B VLM on document parsing, Parakeet and Whisper-turbo transcribe at many times real time on-device, and Kokoro speaks in a 170 MB footprint. So the router should dispatch to specialists, not scale up one generalist. Evidence quality varies by row. Coding, reasoning, OCR, embeddings, reranking and image generation have real leaderboards. SQL, translation, structured extraction and writing have no current benchmarks for September 2026 models, so those matrix cells are reasoned picks rather than measured ones. The single most important local setting is also the easiest to miss: **every current Qwen, Gemma 4 and Granite 4.2 model thinks by default and must be switched to non-thinking for latency-sensitive routes**.

## Hosted general models sort into three tiers, and price no longer tracks intelligence

OpenRouter's live catalog listed **458 text-output models** on 26 September 2026 ([OpenRouter models API](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models)). Three things determine which models fit which tier: price, index score, and how many tokens a model burns per task.

In the frontier tier, **Claude Opus 5.5** (`anthropic/claude-opus-5.5`, released 22 September) has the highest Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score at max effort (**58**). It also leads Humanity's Last Exam (**61.4%**) and SciCode, and ties GPT-6 Astra on Terminal-Bench 4.0 at **59.6%** ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/claude-opus-5-5)). Its weakness is token use: about **119k output tokens per index task**. **GPT-6 Astra** (`openai/gpt-6-astra`, $10/$50) scores 53, uses about **27k tokens per task**, and costs roughly **40% of Fable 5.1 per task** ($3.26 vs $7.63). It also halves GPT-5.6 Sol's hallucination rate ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/benchmarking-gpt-6-astra)). **Claude Fable 5.1** ($10/$50) ties Astra at 53 and on the Coding Agent Index (62), and Anthropic positions it for long-running agentic workflows ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-1)). Opus 5.5 scores higher than Fable 5.1 at less than half the list price. That makes Opus 5.5 the sensible default frontier model, with Fable kept for long autonomous coding runs and Astra used when token efficiency or cost per task matters most.

The mid tier is where most routed traffic should land. **GPT-6 Sol** and **Claude Sonnet 5** both cost $2/$10. OpenAI says Sol makes about half as many factual mistakes as its predecessor ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/22/openai-launches-gpt-6-sol-and-luna/)). Two other mid-tier options are **Gemini 3.8 Flash** ($0.75/$3.75, with audio, video and file input), which Google says beats Opus 5 on three of its published benchmarks ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/09/02/gemini-3-8-flash-launch/)), and **Grok 4.7** (AA Index 46). The fast/cheap tier is crowded and very inexpensive. **GPT-6 Luna** is $0.10/$0.50 with a 1.05M context. **Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite** is $0.30/$2.50 and multimodal. **DeepSeek V4 Flash** is $0.047/$0.094. **Qwen3.7 Flash** is $0.03/$0.13. **GLM-5.3-Flash** is $0.04/$0.50 ([OpenRouter models API](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models)). Among open-weight models, the frontier is **GLM-5.3 (AA 45)** and **Kimi K3 (44)** ([Morph](https://www.morphllm.com/best-open-source-llm)).

Four operational details from the catalog shape router code. OpenRouter publishes **floating aliases** such as `~anthropic/claude-sonnet-latest`, `~openai/gpt-luna-latest` and `~google/gemini-flash-latest`, so the router can pin a tier instead of a version. The **`-pro` OpenAI ids** are the same model with `reasoning.mode=pro`, at the same per-token price. **Sonnet 5 and Fable 5.x do not accept `temperature`**, so the router must remove that parameter for them. And nearly every current model supports `reasoning` and `structured_outputs` ([OpenRouter models API](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models)). Some prices conflict between sources, so treat them with caution: OpenRouter lists Grok 4.7 at $1.60/$4.80 while xAI announced $2/$6 ([x.ai](https://x.ai/news/grok-4-7)), and OpenRouter shows GPT-5.6 Sol at $2/$10 while press coverage gives $4/$20. There is **no Claude Haiku 5, no GPT-6 Terra, no Gemini 3.5 Pro and no Grok 5**. The current Gemini Pro is still `google/gemini-3.1-pro-preview` from February ([Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/gemini-3-5-pro-launch-guide-2026/)).

## Local text runs on two Qwen models, but thinking must be turned off

For text work under about 35B parameters, the Qwen line leads almost every column. **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B** is a mixture-of-experts model with **3B active parameters** and a 262K native context. It reports **SWE-bench Verified 73.4, Terminal-Bench 2.0 51.5, LiveCodeBench v6 80.4, AIME26 92.7 and GPQA 86.0**. On the same card, Gemma 4 31B scores 52.0 on SWE-V ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B)). The dense **Qwen3.8-27B**, released 14 August, raises Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 63.4 to **73.0** and OSWorld-Verified from 63.9 to **84.3** over Qwen3.6-27B. These are vendor numbers reported by a secondary site ([kingy.ai](https://kingy.ai/blog/qwen3-8-27b-specs-benchmarks-local-hardware/)). Simon Willison found it "excellent" as a Pi-framework coding agent. He also found that it defaults to `xhigh` reasoning: one prompt took **21 minutes and 22,276 reasoning tokens** with default reasoning, against 137 seconds with reasoning off, at 15–30 tok/s on an M5 Max ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/16/qwen-38-27b/)).

That finding sets the main local routing rule. Use the 3B-active MoE with `think: false` for interactive answers such as chat, SQL, shell, extraction and quick facts. Reserve thinking, and the dense 27B, for agentic coding and math. Speed depends mostly on the runtime. On an M4 Max, the same 35B-A3B model ran at about **130 tok/s through the MLX Python API, 71 through llama.cpp and 41–48 through Ollama's Q4_K_M build** ([Ante Kapetanovic](https://antekapetanovic.com/blog/qwen3.5-apple-silicon-benchmark/)). Ollama now publishes `-mlx` tags for Qwen3.5, 3.6 and 3.8 ([Ollama qwen3.6](https://ollama.com/library/qwen3.6)), so the app can get MLX-class speed and keep a single Ollama API. No one has measured whether those tags match mlx-lm speed. A separate independent benchmark measured 52 tok/s on an M5 Max for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and 50 for Gemma 4 26B-A4B ([llmcheck](https://llmcheck.net/benchmarks)).

The long-standing heuristic "qwen3-coder:30b is non-thinking and fast" still holds. The model card confirms it "supports only non-thinking mode" and has a 256K context ([HF](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct)). But it is a 2025 model, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking off is a newer model in the same speed class with higher published scores. I found no direct evidence that qwen3-coder is good at SQL or at question answering over long pasted text. Ollama's default `num_ctx` is also far below 256K, so a long-paste route must raise it explicitly.

The router's own classifier is where small models do well. A 2026 benchmark found **format compliance of 98–100%** for 2–4B models, with Qwen3.5-2B at 100%. Strict value accuracy, however, peaked at **75.67%** ([arXiv 2607.16202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16202)). Across 21 models, "every model produces nearly perfect JSON, yet a sizeable fraction of leaf values... are wrong," with gaps of 15–25 points ([arXiv 2604.25359](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25359v1)). For routing, then, valid JSON is solved and the real risk is picking the wrong label. The practical design is `qwen3.5:4b` (3.4 GB) with thinking forced off, an Ollama JSON-schema `format`, few-shot examples, and a labeled evaluation set built from the app's own prompts. Low-confidence cases should fall back to a hosted cheap-tier model.

## Media tasks reward small specialists over big generalists

Document parsing shows this most clearly. On OmniDocBench v1.6, **PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 (0.9B) scores 96.34%, MinerU2.5-Pro (1.2B) 95.75% and GLM-OCR (0.9B, MIT) 95.22%, while Qwen3-VL-235B scores 89.78%** ([Roboflow](https://blog.roboflow.com/best-open-source-ocr-models/)). On olmOCR-bench, the best open models are **dots.mocr (83.9, 3B)** and **LightOnOCR-2-1B (83.2)** ([CodeSOTA](https://www.codesota.com/ocr/benchmark/olmocr-bench)). The hosted options score close to these: Mistral OCR 3 at 81.7 and Gemini 3 Pro at 80.2 ([arXiv 2603.27365](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.27365)). So hosted OCR buys convenience and scale, not better accuracy. `ollama run glm-ocr`, a 2.2 GB download, is the easiest local default ([Ollama glm-ocr](https://ollama.com/library/glm-ocr)).

Speech follows the same pattern, and runtime choice matters as much as model choice. One vendor benchmark on an M5 Pro measured the following ([Soniqo](https://soniqo.audio/benchmarks)):

| Model                               | WER   | RTF                     | Memory |
| ----------------------------------- | ----- | ----------------------- | ------ |
| Qwen3-ASR 1.7B (5-bit, MLX)         | 1.32% | 0.027                   | 1.9 GB |
| WhisperKit large-v3-turbo (Core ML) | 1.71% | 0.084                   | 0.4 GB |
| Parakeet TDT v3 (INT8)              | 2.37% | 0.009 (~110× real time) | 0.9 GB |

Mac speech-to-text has settled into two paths. **Whisper runs through WhisperKit and Core ML on the Neural Engine**, while **Parakeet runs through MLX on the GPU**. One report says MLX Parakeet "monopolized the GPU", which matters when a local LLM is also running ([MacParakeet](https://macparakeet.com/blog/whisper-to-parakeet-neural-engine/)). Parakeet v3 covers 25 European languages; Whisper covers 99 and can translate ([Spokenly](https://spokenly.app/blog/parakeet-vs-whisper)). A good default is Parakeet for English dictation, WhisperKit for multilingual audio or whenever the GPU is busy, and pyannote `community-1` for diarization. Community-1 had the lowest DER in all ten domains of pyannote's own benchmark ([pyannote](https://www.pyannote.ai/blog/community-1)). The cheapest hosted option is `gpt-4o-mini-transcribe` at **$0.003/min** ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). For TTS, **Kokoro-82M runs at RTF 0.17 in about 170 MB**, while CosyVoice3 and Qwen3-TTS trade speed (RTF 0.59–0.79) for voice cloning ([Soniqo](https://soniqo.audio/benchmarks)). The hosted quality leaders are **Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS (Elo 1214)** and Cartesia Sonic 3.5 ([Artificial Analysis TTS](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard)). `mlx-audio` provides a single OpenAI-compatible server for Whisper, Parakeet, Qwen3-ASR, Kokoro and Qwen3-TTS ([mlx-audio](https://github.com/Blaizzy/mlx-audio)).

Images are the one area where hosted models clearly win on quality. On LMArena text-to-image, **GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst leads at 1424**, and the best open-weight model, **Qwen-Image-2.1, ranks #17 at 1228** under a research license that bars commercial use. The image-edit board has the same leader at 1526 ([arena.ai](https://arena.ai/leaderboard/text-to-image); [arena.ai edit](https://arena.ai/leaderboard/image-edit); [MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/21/alibaba-qwen-releases-qwen-image-2-1/)). Locally, **mflux** is the MLX-native engine. It supports Z-Image, FLUX.2 klein 4B/9B, Qwen-Image 2.1, Ideogram 4, fill/inpainting and SeedVR2 upscaling ([mflux](https://github.com/filipstrand/mflux)). FLUX.2 klein 4B renders a 1024² image in **30–40 s on an M1 Max**. The 9B model needs about 29 GB and 85 s even on an M4 Max ([lilting.ch](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/flux2-klein-apple-silicon)). Licensing shapes the local picks. **Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Apache 2.0) is the best commercially usable local editor**, while FLUX.2 dev/9B are non-commercial. Hosted prices at 1K resolution run from about $0.014/MP (FLUX.2 klein 4B) through $0.03 (FLUX.2 pro) and $0.045 (Seedream 5 Pro) to about $0.053 (GPT Image 2 at medium) ([OpenRouter endpoints](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/images/models/openai/gpt-image-2/endpoints); [aifreeapi](https://www.aifreeapi.com/en/posts/openai-image-generation-api-pricing)).

Video generation should be local only as an opt-in, gated by available memory. `mlx-video` runs LTX-2 and Wan 2.1/2.2 ([mlx-video](https://github.com/Blaizzy/mlx-video)). LTX-2.3 needs about **19.4 GB at Q4**, and Wan 2.2 14B fills 32 GB ([note.com](https://note.com/mikai_daichi/n/nab2a5d452f83?hl=en)). On the hosted side, the Artificial Analysis arena ranks **Gemini Omni Flash (1233) and Wan 3.0 (1229)** at the top for video with audio ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/video/leaderboard/text-to-video)). **OpenAI removed the Sora API on 24 September 2026** ([OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)), so any Sora entry in the routing table is now dead. For embeddings, **Qwen3-Embedding-8B scores 70.58 on multilingual MTEB against 58.93 for text-embedding-3-large**, and the 0.6B model still scores 64.33 ([Qwen3-Embedding-8B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-8B)). **Qwen3-Reranker-4B scores 69.76 on MTEB-R against 57.03 for bge-reranker-v2-m3** ([Qwen3-Reranker-4B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Reranker-4B)). Ollama still lacks a rerank endpoint ([PR #7219](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/pull/7219)), so local reranking needs llama.cpp, MLX or sentence-transformers.

## One PKCE-issued OpenRouter key reaches every hosted modality

The OAuth flow needs no backend and no client secret. The Electron main process generates a verifier and computes a **base64url SHA-256 challenge**. It then opens `https://openrouter.ai/auth?callback_url=http://127.0.0.1:<port>/callback&code_challenge=<challenge>&code_challenge_method=S256` (with an optional `key_label` and `workspace_id`) and receives `?code=` on a loopback server. **Localhost callbacks work "on any port"**, which fits an ephemeral-port listener. The one cost is attribution: the app gets a fixed title such as `localhost:51423` and does not appear in rankings ([OpenRouter OAuth PKCE](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/overview/auth/oauth)). The app then exchanges the code with `POST https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/auth/keys` and body `{code, code_verifier, code_challenge_method:"S256"}`, which returns `{key:"sk-or-v1-…", user_id}`. **Codes expire after 10 minutes**, and a bad verifier returns 403 ([Exchange API](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api/api-reference/oauth/exchange-authorization-code-for-api-key.md)). If a loopback server is impractical, a headless variant omits `callback_url` and has the user paste the code; `code_challenge` is required in that mode. The browser flow has **no URL parameter for a spending limit**. Limits exist only on the server-side `POST /auth/keys/code`, and it is unclear who may call that endpoint. After sign-in, the app should store the key with `safeStorage` and show balances from **`GET /api/v1/key`** (`limit_remaining`, `usage_daily` and so on), because `/credits` requires a management key ([Get current key](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api/api-reference/api-keys/get-current-api-key.md)). Attribution needs **`HTTP-Referer` plus `X-OpenRouter-Title`**, and localhost apps must send the title header to be tracked ([App attribution](https://openrouter.ai/docs/app-attribution)).

**`GET /api/v1/models`** is public. Each model object carries `architecture.input_modalities`/`output_modalities`, per-token `pricing` strings (including `image`, `audio`, `input_cache_read` and `web_search`), `supported_parameters`, `context_length` and `top_provider.max_completion_tokens`. It accepts filters on modality, price, age and even Artificial Analysis intelligence and coding indexes ([Get models](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api/api-reference/models/get-models)). By default it returns text-output models only: 458 by default, **628 with `output_modalities=all`**, 57 for image output and 49 for audio input on 26 September. Separate listings exist at `/api/v1/images/models` (55) and `/api/v1/embeddings/models` (33). This lets the router build its capability map at startup rather than hard-coding one, for example by removing `temperature` wherever `supported_parameters` omits it.

Each media type has its own request shape. **Image generation** goes through a dedicated `POST /api/v1/images`, which accepts `{model, prompt, n, resolution:"512"|"1K"|"2K"|"4K", aspect_ratio, quality, output_format, input_references:[{type:"image_url",image_url:{url}}]}` and returns `data[].b64_json` with `usage.cost`. Failed generations are not billed ([Image generation guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/overview/multimodal/image-generation)). The chat path uses `modalities:["image","text"]` with an optional `image_config` and returns images in `message.images[].image_url.url` as data URLs. That path is still in the OpenAPI schema ([Chat completions](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api/api-reference/chat/create-a-chat-completion.md)), but the image guide no longer documents it, so test it before depending on it. A beta `openrouter:image_generation` server tool lets any chat model call an image model ([Server tool](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/features/server-tools/image-generation.md)). **Audio input** is a content part, `{"type":"input_audio","input_audio":{"data":"<base64>","format":"wav"}}`, and it **must be base64; URLs are rejected**. **Audio output** needs `modalities:["text","audio"]`, `audio:{voice,format}` and **`stream:true`**, and arrives as `delta.audio` chunks ([Audio guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/overview/multimodal/audio)). **Video input** uses `{"type":"video_url","video_url":{"url":…}}`, with Gemini-specific quirks: AI Studio accepts only YouTube URLs and Vertex accepts only base64 ([Video guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/overview/multimodal/videos)). Four more endpoints complete the set. `POST /api/v1/embeddings` is OpenAI-compatible. `POST /api/v1/rerank` returns `results[{index, relevance_score}]` ([Rerank API](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api/api-reference/rerank/submit-a-rerank-request.md)). `/audio/speech`, `/audio/transcriptions` and async `/videos` endpoints were announced in July ([OpenRouter blog](https://openrouter.ai/blog/insights/every-modality-one-api/)). Web search is the `:online` suffix or `plugins:[{id:"web"}]`. It uses native search for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Perplexity and xAI models and Exa ($0.007/request) for everything else, and it returns `url_citation` annotations ([Web search](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/features/plugins/web-search)).

## Conclusion

The evidence changes where a desktop router should spend its complexity. Choosing a model within a tier matters less than it used to. Opus 5.5, Astra and Fable sit within five index points of each other, and the cheap tier costs pennies. The decisions that change the user's experience are elsewhere: whether reasoning is on (a 21-minute answer versus a two-minute one), which runtime serves the model (MLX at about 3× Ollama's GGUF speed), which accelerator a speech model occupies (Neural Engine or GPU), and whether a task goes to a sub-2B specialist at all. In practice, the router needs a per-route `reasoning_effort`/`think` setting, capability metadata pulled live from `/api/v1/models`, and a local-first policy for OCR, speech-to-text, TTS, embeddings and classification, where small models match or beat hosted ones.

Two gaps should shape what gets built next. The app's own classifier is the weakest measured link, at roughly 75% strict label accuracy even when the JSON is perfect, so a labeled evaluation set drawn from real pi-gui prompts is worth more than any model swap. And the "inferred" rows (SQL, translation, structured extraction, long-context recall and writing) are where a small in-app evaluation would most improve on this matrix, because no public September 2026 benchmark covers them.
